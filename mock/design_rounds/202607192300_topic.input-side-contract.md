# Topic: the input-side contract (a grammar owes IR, the framework owns no front-end)

**Date:** 2026-07-19
**Phase:** TOPIC
**Scope:** Where the framework's boundary sits on the way in: what a grammar consumer owes, and what it may
assume the framework provides. Continues the round opened by `202607190650_topic.extension-point-contract.md`
and `202607192254_topic.framework-identity.md`.
**Reference:** `mock/research/202607190650_mockspace-procedural-docs-arc/prior_art/` (03 pandoc/djot, 04
racket/scribble, 06 mlir/nanopass).

## The decision

The framework's input contract is only this: **a grammar produces well-formed IR (the shared substrate plus
its own declared family), and the framework owns nothing of the front-end.** A grammar consumer brings its own
lexer and parser in whatever technology fits. The framework offers reusable lex/parse building blocks and a
node **builder** as opt-in libraries, never as a mandatory machine. This is the pandoc-types shape.

Rejected: the framework owning a generic front-end machine parameterised over token kind (a lexer/parser the
consumer merely configures), and the framework compiling a grammar-declaration spec into a front-end
(grammar-as-data). Both are the generation-framework path the arc priced and rejected at this scale.

## Why the prior art leans here, decisively

Every shipped many-input system separates the shared representation from the front-ends, versions the
representation independently, and makes each front-end per-grammar. None ships a mandatory generic front-end.

**Pandoc**, the direct analog (roughly 40 formats in, 60 out, over one AST): the AST ships as a separately
versioned package, `pandoc-types`. Each reader is its own function (`readMan` is 561 lines, its own two-stage
lex-then-parse pipeline), dispatched by an association list. The only thing every reader shares is
`Text.Pandoc.Builder`, a monoid smart-constructor layer for constructing the AST. So the framework is the AST
plus a builder helper; each reader owns its whole front-end.

**Racket `#lang`**: a file picks a reader (bytes to syntax objects). The framework offers reusable reader
scaffolding (`syntax/module-reader`) that a language may use with overridable `#:read`/`#:read-syntax`, or
replace entirely. The `@`-reader is 672 lines in a separate package (`at-exp-lib`), not the core.

**Djot and every Pandoc reader** are hand-written per format. Nobody in the survey generates the front-end.

The two rejected alternatives are the MLIR and nanopass path. MLIR's builtin dialect is two operations
("little builtin, everything customizable"), but its dialect dispatch is trait/interface: open, and
exhaustiveness is uncheckable, which defeats the inclusion-check the output side depends on. nanopass's
grammar-as-data machinery is roughly 4600 lines with documented `extends` exact-name-matching fragility. At a
core of about 15 forms and 30 constructors, the generation framework costs more than the hand-writing it
saves, and it costs checkable exhaustiveness, which nothing else in the survey gives up.

The one caveat that could flip it: if grammars were authored by non-programmers, grammar-as-data would lower
the barrier. The census is all first-party or capable-extension-author work, so the barrier argument is weak.
Pandoc and Racket both assume the reader author is a programmer.

## What this means for the current crates

`vehje-lex` (a hardcoded Rust-shaped tokeniser) and `vehje-syntax` (its parser) are not the framework's front
end under this decision. They become either the first-party general-purpose language consumer's front end, or
opt-in reusable lex/parse building blocks the framework offers without mandating. Which of the two is a
crate-surface question for a later topic. The framework mandates only the IR contract plus a builder for
constructing substrate-plus-family nodes.

## Still open (later topics)
